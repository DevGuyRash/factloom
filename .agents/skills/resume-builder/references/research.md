# Researching the targets

Research should settle a writing or targeting decision. Start from the person's requested roles, level, location, engagement constraints, existing guides, and evidence. For one application, the actual posting is the primary brief. For a reusable variant, investigate the role family across distinct employers; a repost on several boards is one observation. Reuse relevant dated research and refresh what could change the decision.

Read the exact target when supplied, then compare adjacent roles in the relevant industry to distinguish shared capabilities from role-specific credentials, scope, or terminology. Follow work and skills, not title similarity alone. For a general resume, start from the person's established profession and the related work their evidence supports. Keep this comparison small and decision-led; it informs wording and transferable strengths without silently changing the target or creating extra variants.

## Read the work, not just the title

Prefer live employer postings and their linked application pages. Keep the employer, role, requisition or URL, location, posting date when available, access date, and whether the page is live, archived, or unavailable. Open the page: a search snippet or a redirect to a careers index does not establish a current vacancy. Historical postings remain evidence of that historical role, not current demand.

Read for:

- The problems to solve, users or customers served, deliverables, and how success is judged.
- Required versus preferred qualifications, eligibility gates, seniority and ownership, and relevant tools and terminology.
- Differences by role family, employer type, location, and engagement. Separate individual contribution, team leadership, commercial ownership, and customer-facing delivery even when titles overlap.

Use employer hiring guidance, published career ladders, professional or licensing bodies, and occupational databases to resolve specific questions. With sparse intake, these sources identify likely work to ask about and evidence worth recovering. Keep proposed duties or qualifications in questions or clearly labeled suggestions outside the person's facts and resume; only their records or confirmation can establish them. Use the actual ATS vendor's documentation for parser behavior, separating documented behavior from advice or inference. Check employer rules about AI assistance when relevant. Research pay when it affects the target or is requested; keep geography, level, currency, date, and base versus total compensation distinct.

## Learn from the work and hiring experience

Go beyond postings when the writing decision needs it. Look for people who have held comparable roles, their public professional histories, first-person accounts of getting hired, employer or interviewer explanations, interview questions, and examples of the work evaluated. Professional communities, interview-review sites, discussion forums, and other relevant sources can expose expectations a posting leaves implicit. Sites such as Reddit or Glassdoor are possible sources, not a mandatory route or checklist. Start with the unanswered question and adapt searches by role, employer, seniority, location, industry language, and what earlier findings reveal.

Keep source character and limits visible: direct employer guidance, a named practitioner's account, an anonymous report, or commentary. Preserve relevant dates, role/level/geography, and whether it describes an application, interview, offer, or actual work. First-person hiring stories are self-selected and retrospective; record what the writer credits for their success without treating that as a proven hiring cause. Corroborate consequential conclusions with employer criteria or independent relevant accounts. A reported interview question can identify a capability worth demonstrating, not prove every applicant will be asked it. Use legitimately accessible sources and permitted alternatives when access is limited; a snippet establishes only the text actually seen.

Translate findings into decisions: which responsibilities and achievements deserve space, which skills need concrete demonstration, what terminology clarifies the work, and which interview concerns the resume should answer or flag for preparation. Record the useful finding, its confidence, and what it changed; a collection of links is not synthesis. Other people's experience can prompt questions or inform an explicitly synthetic research candidate, but it does not become a genuine applicant's biography. Keep findings that challenge the draft, not only stories that confirm the desired argument.

## Map requirements to evidence

In the guide's Market notes, keep a compact comparison: requirement or outcome, supporting source, the person's fact id and evidence source, and the editorial decision. Distinguish:

- **Supported and visible:** preserve the useful evidence and recognizable terms.
- **Visibility gap:** supported but buried, unclear, or omitted; change emphasis or wording within its factual scope.
- **Transferable:** name the experience actually held and the relevant overlap; another tool or industry is not the same qualification.
- **Evidence gap or fit constraint:** record what is missing or incompatible; a keyword cannot close it.

Compare the resume's strongest proof with the actual work. Priorities depend on the profession: safe practice, teaching, craftsmanship, service, analysis, creative work, commercial results, or technical delivery can matter where supported. Do not import another person's career priorities, or treat technology, revenue, leadership, or a portfolio as universal requirements.

Before writing, record a short target brief in the existing guide: the work and outcomes sought; genuine eligibility gates; the strongest evidence and supported keywords to lead with; material concerns or missing evidence; and the choices this changes in the draft. Show what transfers across adjacent roles and what remains specific to one. Prefer the employer's material, then professional or licensing authorities and occupational sources for that geography. Distinguish employer requirements from profession-wide patterns. Treat recruiter advice as advice and unsupported screening claims as hypotheses. Investigate contradictions rather than collecting more similar postings.

A source is useful when its relevance, date, authority, and effect on a decision are clear. A prestige brand, long source list, or model-generated score does not establish research quality. Explain which findings changed the target, content selection, terminology, or ordering, and which did not.

## Enough to write

Choose sources to cover the decisions that differ across the requested targets, rather than completing a posting quota. Call a requirement recurring only when distinct employers support it, and state the sample's limits. Investigate conflicting examples or an unrepresented target before generalizing. Stop when further relevant sources no longer change the argument, priority requirements, variant boundaries, or material gaps. With sparse or inaccessible sources, make the narrower supported recommendation and say what remains uncertain; continue drafting from known facts.

Record the research date, source links, coverage and limits, role-specific priorities, and what they changed in the guide. Findings common to sibling variants can share a linked research note in the person's private files. A new variant needs a different hiring argument or evidence selection, not merely another title or a different keyword order. Refresh when the target changes or new evidence challenges the guide; its age is a prompt to check, not a reason to repeat all the research.

## Occupational sources

The [U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET overview](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/eta/onet) describes occupation-specific tasks and worker requirements. [O*NET's related-occupation method](https://www.onetcenter.org/dictionary/31.0/mssql/related_occupations.html) considers work, knowledge, and titles. Use these to investigate transferable capabilities, with the appropriate geography's equivalent sources where needed; similarity between occupations neither establishes an applicant's competence nor replaces the actual employer's criteria.
