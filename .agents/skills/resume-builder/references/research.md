# Researching the targets

Research for this person, for these targets, now. Advice about resumes in general goes stale, differs by field, level, and country, and is often written to sell something; what employers in this person's field are asking for this month is what counts. Use whatever the host gives you (web search, the browser, the person's own sources), and keep the source and date of everything you rely on, in the variant's guide ("Market notes") or in `evidence.md`.

Directions that tend to pay off, as starting points rather than a list to complete:

- **The work itself**: what people in the target role do day to day, which tools and methods they use, and what they are measured on (occupational databases such as O*NET describe tasks and tools for many occupations).
- **Current postings**: a broad sample (twenty or more, across the boards the person uses and employers' own career pages), read for the titles in use, the requirements that recur, the words employers use for the same skills, and what separates senior from junior postings.
- **What good looks like at each level**: published career ladders, professional bodies, and licensing boards, so the resume claims the scope the person's facts support.
- **How the field is changing**: trade press, conference programs, regulation, employer reports; what a reader this year will be looking for that they were not a few years ago.
- **Translating a past field**: the target field's words for what the person did elsewhere (military crosswalks, for example, map service roles to civilian ones).
- **Gatekeepers**: credentials, licenses, clearances, or portfolios that decide entry, and what the person can truthfully show for each.
- **Conventions**: what a resume looks like in this field and country (an academic CV, a federal resume, a portfolio-first field), and how its readers screen: applicant systems, AI screening, recruiters, hiring managers, and what each rewards or penalizes now.
- **Anything else** a person hiring for this role would want to know that nothing above covers: ask that question every time.

Research shapes what the resume leads with, how it is organized, and the words it uses for true claims. It never adds a claim: a requirement the person cannot truthfully show is a gap to tell them about, with what would close it.
